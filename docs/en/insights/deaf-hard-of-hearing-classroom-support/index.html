Classroom Support

Supporting a Deaf or Hard-of-Hearing Student in the Inclusive Classroom

A deaf or hard-of-hearing student's success in the inclusive classroom usually depends more on simple environmental and communication accommodations than on full reliance on a device or sign language interpreter.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: September 5, 2026 · 5 min read

How Do You Set Up the Classroom Environment and Seating?

Seat the student where they can clearly see the teacher's face, away from sources of noise like a window or fan. Avoid talking while writing on the board or turning your back to the student.

Good lighting on the speaker's face helps a student who relies on lip-reading or facial expressions to understand part of the content.

How Do You Communicate With the Student During Class?

Speak at a natural pace without exaggerating lip movement, and rephrase a sentence differently if it wasn't understood rather than repeating it the same way. Write key words or instructions on the board as additional visual support.

If a sign language interpreter or a personal FM system is available, coordinate in advance when it will be used and where the student sits, in a way that doesn't isolate them from their peers.

How Do You Ensure Fair Participation and Assessment?

Make sure the student knows who is speaking in a group discussion - fast, multi-voice discussions are among the hardest situations for them. A simple signal or periodic summary of what was said helps them keep up.

When assessing, separate measuring the academic skill from measuring listening skill; written or illustrated instructions give a more accurate picture of the student's real understanding.

How Do You Support Communication Beyond the Classroom?

Communication breaks down most often in unstructured moments - the hallway, the playground, a fire drill - not during planned lessons. Make sure alerts and announcements have a visual equivalent, and brief specials teachers (art, sports, music) the same way as the homeroom teacher.

Check in with the student about which communication method they actually prefer in different settings; a student who lip-reads well one-on-one may still need an interpreter or captions in a noisy assembly.

Note: This content is educational guidance and does not replace follow-up with an audiologist or the student's individual support plan.
