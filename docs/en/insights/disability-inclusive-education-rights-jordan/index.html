Rights and Legislation

A Child With a Disability's Rights to Inclusive Education in Jordan

Inclusion starts with knowing that education is a right, not a favor. This article explains, in practical language, what Jordanian legal protection means, and how families and schools can turn the legal text into clear, actionable, trackable requests.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

What Does the Law Protect?

The Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities No. 20 of 2017 prohibits excluding a person from an educational institution because of disability, and the Child Rights Law No. 17 of 2022 affirms a child with a disability's right to general education and inclusion in educational institutions.

This aligns with Article 24 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, which ties the right to education to an inclusive system, non-discrimination, and providing necessary support within the general system.

What Do Reasonable Accommodations Mean?

These are appropriate adjustments a particular student needs to access learning and assessment on an equal footing, such as extra time, material in an accessible format, an alternative communication method, or a seating adjustment.

Accommodations don't automatically mean lowering the value of learning; they mean removing a specific barrier while preserving a clear educational goal, then reviewing the adjustment's effectiveness with data.

What Are the Practical Steps for Families and Schools?

Write the need, the barrier, the impact, and the proposed adjustment on a single page, and attach any supporting educational notes or relevant reports. Ask for a written response and a date to review implementation.

Keep the conversation centered on the child's participation and progress, and document what was agreed, who is responsible, and the timeframe. If progress stalls, escalate to school administration and the relevant official bodies according to established procedures.

What Do You Do If You Feel the Right Isn't Being Applied?

If you feel your child's right to inclusion isn't actually being applied, start with simple written documentation: the date of the situation, exactly what happened, and who was present. A short email or message through the school's official communication app is enough - what matters is that the communication is in writing, not just verbal.

Ask for a formal meeting with whoever has decision-making authority at the school, not just the teacher, and present the problem as a specific situation with a date and location, not a general complaint. Propose one practical solution instead of just presenting the problem and leaving the solution open.

If the situation isn't resolved after the meeting, calmly escalate to the relevant education directorate, based on the same written documentation you started with. Escalation built on specific facts and clear dates is far more effective than an emotional complaint without details.

Key References: Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities No. 20 of 2017 · Child Rights Law No. 17 of 2022
