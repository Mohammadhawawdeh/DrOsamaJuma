Attention and Organization

Classroom Strategies to Support a Student's Attention and Organization

A classroom environment can reduce the burden on attention and working memory. The goal is to make what's required clear, reduce unnecessary distractions, and provide movement and feedback in an organized way.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

How Do You Break a Long Task Down for the Student?

Present one or two steps at a time, and use a short list the student can track their own completion against. Make sure the student knows how to start, since difficulty starting can sometimes look like refusing the task.

Have the required materials ready, set a realistic time, and use a visual example of the expected outcome.

How Do You Organize Time and Movement Breaks?

Use a visual timer or a cue before a transition, and allow short, movement breaks tied to a clear plan. Tasks can be distributed between sitting and movement instead of requiring the student to stay still for a long period.

Choose a seating location that reduces distractions while keeping the student engaged and not isolated.

How Do You Reinforce and Track Progress With Simple Data?

Give feedback close to the target behavior, and focus on what worked. Record work duration or the number of steps completed to compare the effect of strategies.

If the plan isn't working, review the task's difficulty, how it's presented, the time, and the environment before assuming the problem is motivation.

An Example: Applying an Immediate Reinforcement System

Instead of settling for a general assessment at the end of the day (“you had a good day”), use an immediate, specific reinforcement system: when the student completes a step of the task, reinforce them immediately with a statement that describes exactly what they did (“well done, you finished the first paragraph on time”), not just general praise.

Tie reinforcement to small, verifiable steps within minutes, not hours: completing part of the task, keeping their turn in a discussion, or returning to the task alone after a distraction. The shorter the gap between the behavior and the reinforcement, the clearer the connection is for the student.

Over time, gradually widen the distance between reinforcement moments, from every step to every few steps, instead of continuing immediate reinforcement indefinitely or stopping it suddenly.

Note: This content is general awareness/educational material. Determining the most appropriate support requires understanding the student's needs and learning environment, and collaboration with the relevant team.
