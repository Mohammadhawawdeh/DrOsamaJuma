Assistive Technology

Assistive Technology at School: From Choosing the Tool to Measuring Its Benefit

Assistive technology can be as simple as a pencil grip or as advanced as a screen reader. Its value isn't in its price, but in its ability to increase access, independence, and participation in a real task.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

Key References: Global Report on Assistive Technology – WHO and UNICEF

Where Do You Start: The Task or the Tool?

Identify what the student wants to accomplish, where the barrier occurs, and what tools are actually available. A screen reader is useless if files aren't readable, and the right chair needs an accessible path, desk, and restroom.

Involve the student, family, and teacher in the trial; WHO and UNICEF recommendations emphasize the importance of involving users and families in the process of accessing technology.

Why Start With the Least Complex Tool?

Compare more than one option in real situations, and evaluate accuracy, time, effort, and student acceptance. A simple solution may succeed more than an advanced device that needs support that isn't available.

Don't buy the tool before thinking through training, updates, maintenance, storage, and who's responsible for support when it breaks down.

How Do You Measure the Impact of Assistive Technology?

Set an indicator before use, such as the number of words read independently, engagement duration, or the number of tasks completed. Re-measure after a period of stable use.

If no improvement appears, check whether the tool, training, materials, and environment are a good fit. The problem may be in the system of use, not the student.

How Do You Train Staff and Students to Actually Use the Tool?

A device that arrives without training becomes a drawer item within a month. Budget training time for the teacher, the student, and at least one peer, and schedule a check-in two weeks after introduction - that's when real usage problems surface, not on day one.

Keep a one-page 'if it breaks' guide next to the device: who to call, what the backup plan is for that class period, and how long a repair typically takes. Students lose confidence fast when a tool fails and no one has a plan.

Note: This content is general awareness/educational material. Determining the most appropriate support requires understanding the student's needs and learning environment, and collaboration with the relevant team.
