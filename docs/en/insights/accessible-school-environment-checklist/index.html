School Accessibility

A School Accessibility Checklist: More Than Just a Ramp

Accessibility is a chain that starts at the road and gate and doesn't end at the classroom. A student might reach the building but be unable to use the restroom, read the instructions, participate in the lab, or evacuate safely.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

Key References: UNICEF: Planning, Monitoring, and Evaluation for Inclusive Education · The Higher Council: Plans and Strategies

Is the Path and Facilities Actually Accessible?

Check the path from the entry point to the gate, classroom, lab, library, playground, and restroom. Review the slope, width, doors, handrails, surfaces, obstacles, and lighting.

Test the path with real users, because a single matching element on its own doesn't guarantee the whole journey is accessible.

Are Information and Communication Accessible to All Students?

Provide clear signage, good contrast, readable digital formats, and audio or visual alternatives when needed. Review announcements, assignments, and learning platforms, not just textbooks.

Make sure staff know how to request an interpreter or an accessible material, and that the student doesn't have to re-explain their need every time.

Is There an Emergency Plan and Periodic Review?

Include students with disabilities in evacuation, alarm, and assembly point plans, and identify backup support when the elevator breaks down or power is cut.

Rank barriers by impact and severity, and assign a responsible person, a date, and a budget. Re-check periodically and after any change to the building or schedule.

How Do You Turn the Checklist Into a Budgeted Plan?

Split findings into three tiers: no-cost fixes (moving furniture, adjusting signage height, reserving a seat), low-cost fixes (a portable ramp, better lighting, a laminated visual schedule), and capital fixes (widening a doorway, installing a lift). Start the no-cost tier immediately - it doesn't need budget approval and shows the family the school is acting.

For the capital tier, get at least one written quote so the fix has a real cost attached, not just a wish. A school board is far more likely to approve a ramp with a JOD 400 quote next to it than a vague request to 'improve access.'

Note: This content is general awareness/educational material. Determining the most appropriate support requires understanding the student's needs and learning environment, and collaboration with the relevant team.
