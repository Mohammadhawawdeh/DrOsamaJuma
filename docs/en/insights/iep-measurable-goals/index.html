Individualized Education Plan

How to Write Measurable IEP Goals

The strength of an IEP isn't measured by its number of pages, but by the clarity of its goals and the team's ability to track progress. A good goal specifies the required behavior or skill, the conditions, the performance level, and the timeframe.

By Dr. Osama Juma — PhD in Special Education, The University of Jordan · Published: August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

Why Start From a Clear Baseline?

Before writing the goal, describe what the student can do now based on observation, a work sample, or classroom data. A baseline prevents choosing vague goals and helps the team know the real extent of improvement.

Instead of the phrase “needs to improve communication,” state how many times the student asks for help or answers a question in a specific situation.

What Are the Components of a Measurable Goal?

A goal needs a specific skill, a clear context, a success criterion, and a review timeframe. Example: given a three-step classroom task, the student will complete two steps independently in four out of five attempts over eight weeks.

Avoid words like “understands” or “improves” without observable evidence. Use verbs like chooses, requests, reads, organizes, participates, or completes.

How Do You Track the Goal and Make Decisions?

Decide from the start who collects the data, how often, and how it will be presented to the family. Follow-up doesn't need complex tools; a short, consistent log tied to the goal is often enough.

At review time, ask: has progress happened? What strategy is most effective? Do we need to adjust the goal or the support method?

A Worked Example: From a Vague Goal to a Measurable One

A goal like “improve reading skills” can't actually be measured because it doesn't specify anything clearly observable. Turn it into a wording that specifies the behavior, the condition, and the criterion: “The student will read a 100-word passage with 90% accuracy over 3 consecutive attempts, with simple visual support, by the end of the term.”

Notice the difference: the new goal specifies what (reading a text of a defined length), at what accuracy (90%), under what condition (with visual support), and by when (end of term). This lets the teacher and family know, just two weeks into implementation, whether the current pace is enough to reach the goal on time or needs an early adjustment - rather than waiting until the end of the term to discover a delay.

The practical formula for any measurable goal answers five questions: who is the student, exactly what behavior is targeted, under what condition or support it will be carried out, by what quantitative criterion success will be measured, and by what date it will be reviewed.

Note: This content is general awareness/educational material. Determining the most appropriate support requires understanding the student's needs and learning environment, and collaboration with the relevant team.
